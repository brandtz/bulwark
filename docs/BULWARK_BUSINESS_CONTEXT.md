# BULWARK — The Business Behind the Tool

> A plain-English briefing for anyone helping shape the **operating business**
> that will use the Bulwark software — not the software product itself.
>
> If you are reading this to write a business plan, a go-to-market strategy,
> a pricing model, a hiring plan, or an investor narrative for the *contracting
> company* that will run on Bulwark, this is your starting point. Everything
> technical has been stripped out on purpose.

---

## 1. The One-Sentence Version

A small, licensed general-contracting business in Eastern Oregon that hardens
homes against wildfire — replacing siding, roofing, eaves, vents, and decking
to the standards insurers now demand — and hands every homeowner a sealed
compliance package their insurance company will accept on the first try.

The Bulwark software is the operational backbone that lets this contractor
run more jobs, with fewer mistakes, and produce paperwork that closes the
insurance loop. It is **not** the product the contractor sells. The
**retrofit work and the compliance package** are the products.

---

## 2. Why This Business Exists Right Now

Three forces are colliding in the rural West and creating an unusual
opportunity:

1. **Insurers are non-renewing wildfire-zone policies en masse.** Carriers
   in Oregon, California, Colorado, and Washington are dropping or refusing
   to renew homeowner policies on properties with wood siding, asphalt
   shingles, open eaves, or non-ember-resistant vents in mapped hazard
   zones. Homeowners who cannot get insurance cannot keep their mortgage.

2. **State and county codes now spell out exactly what "hardened" means.**
   Oregon's wildfire hardening rules name the materials, the clearances,
   and the documentation. There is no ambiguity about what passes.

3. **There is almost no organized supply of contractors who do this work
   end-to-end** and deliver a clean insurer-ready compliance package. Most
   roofers do roofs. Most siding crews do siding. Almost no one quarterbacks
   the whole hardening retrofit and produces the paperwork that lets the
   insurer rebind the policy.

The result: a homeowner in a hazard zone whose policy is being non-renewed
has a problem with a deadline, a regulatory definition of the fix, and very
few people to call. Demand is **regulatory, urgent, and geographically
concentrated**.

## 3. Who the Customers Actually Are

The business will earn revenue from three distinct customer types. The
software treats all three as first-class, and the business plan should too.

### 3a. Homeowners in Hazard Zones (primary, day one)
The person whose policy is about to lapse. They are motivated, often
panicked, and almost always need three things at once: a clear bid, a
schedule, and a piece of paper their insurer will accept. Most are not
shopping on price — they are shopping on certainty that the job will
result in a renewable policy.

### 3b. Homeowners Associations (HOAs)
A single decision-maker who needs to bring 30, 60, or 200 homes into
compliance on a coordinated timeline, often ahead of a master-policy
renewal. HOA work is higher-volume per sales cycle, lower per-unit margin,
and rewards a contractor who can sequence dozens of properties without
losing track of which house got which scope.

### 3c. Insurance Carriers and Agents (channel partners, sometimes payers)
Carriers want their book of business to keep its policies. They are
beginning to fund or subsidize hardening work to keep homes insurable.
A contractor who can be trusted to deliver the work *and* the
compliance documentation becomes a referral conduit — the agent sends
the homeowner directly to Bulwark, and the carrier accepts the
compliance package without a fight.

> The same job lives in all three relationships. The homeowner signs the
> contract. The HOA may coordinate access. The insurer is the audience for
> the final paperwork. The business plan needs to address all three
> audiences distinctly.

## 4. What the Contractor Actually Sells

There are two products. Treat them as separate revenue lines.

### Product 1 — The Retrofit Work
Fixed-price packages, sized to what the assessment found:

- **Tier 1 — Roofing only.** Re-roof to Class A fire-rated material.
- **Tier 2 — Siding + roofing.** Re-clad to non-combustible or
  ignition-resistant siding, plus the Tier 1 roof.
- **Tier 3 — Full hardening.** Roof, siding, enclosed eaves,
  ember-resistant vents, deck upgrade if applicable, and a 0–5 ft
  non-combustible defensible zone around the structure.

Each tier maps to a defined scope, a known materials list, and a labor
estimate that the field lead can sanity-check on site in minutes.

### Product 2 — The Compliance Package (the upsell, and the moat)
A signed, sealed PDF the homeowner can hand to their insurer. It contains
the scope of work, the materials used (with fire-rating spec sheets),
permit numbers, inspection sign-offs, before/after photos, and a
GC-signed certification statement.

This document is the **reason the business is defensible**. Any roofer
can replace a roof. Very few can hand the homeowner the artifact the
insurance company actually wants. Customers will pay a premium for the
guarantee that the paperwork will be accepted.

Whether this is bundled into the job price or invoiced separately is a
business-plan decision. It is the highest-leverage pricing question in
the whole model.

## 5. How the Work Actually Flows

A complete engagement runs through six stages. The business plan should
treat each as a cost center and a time budget.

1. **Lead.** Inbound from non-renewal letters, HOA outreach, insurance
   agent referrals, or proactive prospecting against parcel + hazard-zone
   data. Each lead has an outreach status the team tracks until it
   converts or is killed.

2. **Assessment.** Field lead visits the property, walks through a
   structured checklist (siding material and square footage per face,
   roof material and age, eave style, vent type, deck material, access
   notes). The checklist automatically flags what's non-compliant and
   produces a recommended upgrade list. Target: under 60 minutes on
   site.

3. **Quote.** From the recommended upgrades the team builds a line-item
   quote — labor, materials, margin — and sends a client-facing PDF.
   Quote status is tracked through accepted, declined, or revised.

4. **Work Order & Subs.** Accepted quotes become work orders. The job is
   broken into trades (roofing, siding, carpentry) and each trade is
   assigned to a subcontractor with a schedule and materials notes. Subs
   see only their slice of the job — no financials.

5. **Compliance Documentation.** As the job proceeds the team logs permit
   numbers, inspection sign-offs, and before/after photos. When work is
   complete the system assembles the compliance package and the GC signs
   it. The homeowner downloads it.

6. **Invoice & Payment.** Invoice is generated, payment is tracked, and
   the job closes.

The business-plan question that matters most here: **how many of these
end-to-end engagements can one field lead and a small bench of subs run
per week** without quality slipping? That number is the ceiling on
revenue until the second crew is hired.

## 6. Who Does the Work — The Operating Team

| Role | Who | What they own |
|---|---|---|
| Field lead / licensed GC | Drew | Property assessments, sub coordination, client relationships in Eastern Oregon, on-site quality. The face of the business to the homeowner. |
| Operations / admin | Matthew | Client records, billing, compliance document assembly, vendor relationships, scheduling, the books. Not in the field. |
| Capital / insurance liaison (possible) | Jeff | Funding for working capital and materials, connections into the insurance industry to open the carrier-referral channel. |
| Subcontractors | A bench of 3–6 trade crews | Roofing, siding, carpentry, decks, vents. Paid per job. No employees on the books at MVP. |

A separate co-venture entity (distinct from Brandtworks-Enterprises LLC,
which owns the software) is the likely vehicle to operate the contracting
business with Drew and Jeff. The business plan should assume this
separation: **the software company and the contracting company are
related but distinct entities.**

## 7. Geography & Sequencing

- **Year 1: Eastern Oregon.** Concentrated geography, single licensing
  jurisdiction, dense pockets of non-renewal letters, achievable drive
  times for a single field lead.
- **Year 2: Add the next Oregon county and pilot a second crew.** Decide
  whether the second crew is a hired employee or a partnered GC running
  the same playbook.
- **Year 3+: Cross-state.** California, Colorado, Washington each have
  parallel non-renewal dynamics and hardening codes. Each new state
  requires licensure but does not require rebuilding the operational
  model.

---

## 8. The Numbers That Drive the Plan

These are the levers a business plan or financial model should put names
on. The software is built to surface every one of them, but the
business-plan author has to decide what the targets are.

- **Average job size**, separately for Tier 1, Tier 2, Tier 3.
- **Gross margin per tier** after materials and sub costs.
- **Jobs per field-lead-week** — the throughput ceiling.
- **Assessment-to-quote conversion**, **quote-to-job conversion**.
- **Days from accepted quote to compliance package delivered** — this is
  the metric the homeowner cares about and the insurer rewards.
- **Working-capital float** — materials are paid before the homeowner
  pays the final invoice. The gap is the financing problem.
- **Customer acquisition cost** broken down by channel: insurance-agent
  referrals, HOA wins, direct-to-homeowner outreach.
- **Compliance-document attach rate** — what fraction of jobs include
  the upsell, and what does it add per job.

## 9. Where Competition Comes From

- **The single-trade contractors who already serve the area.** They are
  cheaper for a one-product job (just a roof, just siding) but cannot
  deliver the full retrofit or the compliance package.
- **Out-of-region disaster-chasers.** They follow wildfire seasons and
  bid aggressively, but have no standing relationship with local
  inspectors or insurance agents. Their compliance paperwork is often
  thin.
- **Insurance-funded preferred-vendor programs.** These will appear over
  the next 24 months as carriers stand up their own networks. Becoming
  one of those preferred vendors *is the strategy*, not the threat.
- **DIY homeowners.** A real factor on rural properties. The defence is
  the compliance package — a DIY job rarely produces paperwork the
  insurer will accept.

## 10. The Risks That Can Kill the Business

In rough order of likelihood:

1. **Licensure timing.** The whole plan presumes Drew completes his GC
   licensure on schedule. A delay pushes revenue into the next quarter.
2. **Working capital.** Retrofit jobs front-load materials cost. Without
   a line of credit or an outside backer, throughput is capped at "what
   the last payment funded."
3. **Sub bench depth.** A single sub flaking on a roofing assignment in
   August can stall three jobs at once. The business plan needs a
   contingency bench, not a primary bench.
4. **Insurance behaviour shift.** If carriers stop non-renewing
   (unlikely in this decade, but possible) the urgency that drives
   inbound demand softens. The HOA and insurance-channel relationships
   are the hedge.
5. **Regulatory change.** Hardening standards are still being refined.
   A favourable revision is upside; an unfavourable one (e.g. an
   approved material gets de-listed) means re-quoting in-flight jobs.
6. **Wildfire season disruption.** Smoke, evacuation orders, and burn
   bans can pause field work for weeks. The schedule has to absorb it.

## 11. What the Software Frees the Business to Do

The Bulwark application is the operational backbone — what it removes
from the operating business's plate, in plain terms:

- The field lead never has to remember which property got which scope.
  Every assessment, quote, work order, and photo lives on one timeline
  per property.
- The admin never has to assemble a compliance PDF by hand. The
  document is built from data the team already entered as the job
  progressed.
- Subs see only what they need to do their job. Homeowners see only
  status and their documents. No accidental sharing of margin or
  pricing.
- Pipeline, revenue, and job-status visibility are one screen, not
  a stack of spreadsheets.
- The same operating model is exportable: a second crew, a second
  county, a partnered GC running the same playbook on their own client
  list — all share the same backbone without forking the process.

This last point is the long-term strategic note. The contracting
business and the software it runs on are joined at the hip in Year 1.
By Year 3, the software is independently licensable to other GCs
running the same retrofit niche in other states — but that is the
software company's revenue line, not the contracting company's, and
should be treated as a separate venture in any business plan written
for the contracting side.

## 12. Useful Inputs the Business Plan Author May Want

Items the business plan should figure out, source, or quantify — the
software does not answer these:

- The list of insurance carriers actively non-renewing in target ZIPs.
- HOA inventories in target counties, with master-policy renewal dates.
- Public parcel + hazard-zone data sources for prospecting.
- Pricing of Class A roofing materials and fiber-cement siding from
  one or two regional suppliers (the difference between a 22% and a
  35% gross margin lives here).
- A go/no-go bar for taking on a job: minimum size, maximum drive time,
  acceptable access conditions.
- Standard-contract terms: deposit, progress payments, retention,
  warranty length.
- Working-capital target before Year 1 launch.
- Insurance/bonding the operating entity itself will need to carry
  (general liability, workers' comp where applicable, completed-ops).
- Whether the compliance package is sold as a separate SKU or bundled.

---

## 13. North-Star Outcomes (Year 1)

A successful Year 1 looks like this. These are the headline numbers a
business plan should defend.

- The contracting entity is legally formed, licensed, bonded, insured.
- A defined sub bench is signed up across roofing, siding, and
  carpentry with at least one backup per trade.
- A target number of homes hardened in Eastern Oregon (the business
  plan author picks the number) at a defined average ticket and
  defined gross margin.
- A first formal referral relationship with at least one insurance
  agent or carrier representative.
- At least one HOA contract closed and delivered.
- Every job closed with an accepted compliance package — zero insurer
  rejections.
- A reproducible playbook (sales, assessment, quote, build, document)
  that a second crew could follow in Year 2 without reinventing it.
